01

What we found, before we asked you anything

Everything below came from your public storefront and from public regulation. Three of the six are flagged for your own advisers rather than answered here.

1

United States orders are carrying duty nobody has priced

On 29 August 2025 the United States removed the $800 de minimis exemption. Every parcel you send to a US customer now needs a tariff classification, a country-of-origin determination, and a carrier that can actually claim CUSMA — postal channels can no longer do so, commercial couriers can.

Tea grown in Asia and blended in Canada has to clear a tariff-shift test to be CUSMA-originating. Steepware made in China answers differently. Only a customs broker can rule on that, and we never will. What we can do is hold the data a broker needs — heading, origin, value, per-order duty — and show you what the answer is worth before you pay for it.

What we would buildHS heading and country of origin as first-class fields on the product master, landed cost per SKU per destination, and a United States margin view that shows duty as a line rather than a mystery.
2

Two storefronts, one catalogue, maintained twice

Every product on firebellytea.ca carries the tag CA Market and a SKU beginning CA-. Your catalogue is already partitioned by market at the data level. What does not exist is anything that reconciles the two sides: a price change, a new bundle, a description edit or a promotion is done twice, and stock truth lives in a third place at the warehouse.

On the day we looked, the Canadian store carried 47 products in 156 variants. Whether the American catalogue matches is a question you can only answer today by exporting both and comparing by hand.

What we would buildOne product and price master — SKUs, costs, bundle recipes, tax status, heading, origin — that pushes to both storefronts and reconciles against the warehouse, so a price change is one edit.
3

A quarter of your buyable variants were out of stock

41 of 156 variants were unavailable on the Canadian storefront when we measured it. For most retailers that is a merchandising number. For a subscription business it is a retention number: a subscriber whose tea is not there is a subscriber who cancels.

Reorder points that are driven by trailing sales cannot see that. Reorder points driven by committed subscriber shipments can.

What we would buildLot-level stock with the warehouse as a synced location, and cover calculated against subscription commitments rather than sales velocity.
4

Certified organic is a chain of custody, not a badge

The claim on the pack is an obligation: every lot needs a supplier certificate that was valid on the date of purchase, produceable on demand. Tea is also agricultural — harvests are seasonal and lead times are long, so a certificate that lapses is not a same-week problem.

What we would buildA supplier and lot register with certificate expiry alerts ninety days out, purchase orders, and landed cost captured per lot — which is what makes every margin figure downstream honest.
5

Bundles put zero-rated tea and taxable steepware in one box

Loose-leaf tea is zero-rated as a basic grocery in Canada; steepware is not. Your own FAQ says so. But several of your best-selling products are bundles that contain both, and a mixed package falls under the gift-basket rules in CRA GST/HST Memorandum 4-3 — where the answer is not simply to split by value.

Your accountant rules on this, not us. What we can do is put the composition of every bundle in front of them in a form they can act on in an afternoon.

What we would buildTax status per SKU, explicit bundle composition, and a monthly reconciliation your accountant can read without asking anyone a question.
6

Business-to-business is three inboxes and a marketplace

Wholesale runs through a Faire listing and two email addresses. Faire owns the retailer relationship and takes its cut — a fair price for discovery and an expensive one for a reorder. The two inboxes have no account pricing, no reorder cadence and nobody assigned, so a hotel that ordered in March is not asked again in September unless somebody happens to remember.

Corporate gifting is worse in one specific way: it is a two-month business, run out of an email address, in the eight weeks of the year when everyone is busiest.

What we would buildA B2B ordering portal with account-level pricing and one-click reorder, plus enough of a pipeline to run the gifting season on a calendar rather than on memory.

03

What we deliberately do not touch

Saying this out loud early matters more than anything else in this document.

  • Not your storefront, and not your checkout. They work, your customers like them, and replacing them would be a bad idea for reasons that have nothing to do with software.
  • Not your subscription billing. We read the data. We never touch the charge.
  • Not your warehouse. Your third-party logistics partner keeps the building and the boxes. We hold the truth about cost, lot and certificate.
  • Not an enterprise resource planning system. This is six modules that answer six questions, and it stays that size unless you ask otherwise.
  • No customs, tax or advertising rulings. We surface the data and name the question. Your broker, your accountant and your counsel answer it.

05

What we would need from you

None of it is unusual, and none of it is needed before the first conversation.

  • A product export from each storefront, and read access to both.
  • Whatever supplier cost sits behind the catalogue today — a spreadsheet is fine, and is what we usually get.
  • The last three months of warehouse invoices, and a recent carrier invoice.
  • The organic certificates you hold, however they are currently filed.
  • An introduction to your accountant, and to your customs broker if you have one.
  • One person on your side who can answer questions in the same week they are asked.
